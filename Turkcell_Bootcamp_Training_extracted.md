Edge Data Centers: Bringing Computing Closer
As modern applications demand ultra-low latency, traditional centralized data centers—often located far from end
users—are no longer sufficient for real-time processing needs. This gave rise to edge data centers, which are:
• Smaller, decentralized facilities located near the source of data generation (e.g., in cities, factories, or cell towers).
• Used to process data locally before sending it to central or cloud data centers, reducing response times and
bandwidth usage.
• Essential for technologies like:
⚬Autonomous vehicles
⚬Smart cities
⚬Industrial IoT
⚬Augmented/Virtual Reality (AR/VR)
⚬5G networks
• These centers support localized workloads and ensure fast, reliable service with minimal delay

## Slide 18

2.Site Selection
Definiton
When housing a data center in an existing building, several design issues must be considered to choose the
best location. Careful planning is essential to assure that a location will meet not only immediate needs, but
future needs as well. In the event that a building or area must be built to house the data center, there are
even more considerations. The build-to-suit option typically offers more flexibility than utilizing an existing
area, but careful planning is still essential. Looking ahead and planning the site and layout with forethought
can save tremendous amounts of time, money, and aggravation. Poor planning often means costly upgrading,
retrofitting, or relocating.
This section outlines key considerations for selecting a data center location, whether it's a:
• Greenfield site (new build),
• Existing building repurposed as a data center,
• Or part of a consolidation/closure evaluation.

## Slide 26

2.Site Selection
Site Selection Considerations: Natural Hazards
• Seismic Activity
Introduction
Seismic activity refers to earthquakes, typically caused by geological faults or volcanic activity. These events can
range from minor vibrations to severe, destructive shocks lasting over 20 seconds. Earthquakes pose a serious risk to
data center structures and operations.
Recommendations
• It is recommended to avoid seismically active areas when selecting a site for a data center.
• If such areas cannot be avoided, seismic-rated equipment supports and structural systems must be installed in
accordance with the requirements of the local Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ).
• All infrastructure, including ITE cabinets and racks, should be designed and anchored based on the expected
seismic load at the site.
• The building structure will require stronger reinforcement if located in a seismic zone.
• If not mandated by the AHJ, it is still advisable to consult with a structural engineer to ensure the facility meets
suitable seismic performance standards.

## Slide 31

2.Site Selection
Site Selection Considerations: Natural Hazards
• Volcanic Activity
Introduction
Volcanic activity is a risk primarily near geological faults, but active volcanoes can also occur in other regions.
The hazards related to volcanoes include eruptions and their resulting impacts such as lava flows, ash fall,
lahars (volcanic mudflows), and flooding.
Recommendations
• Data centers should be located outside the immediate hazard or buffer zone of any active volcano.
• Each volcano has its own unique risk zone—even when multiple volcanoes are close together, their
behavior and risk profiles may differ.
• Local hazard maps for each volcano in the area should be reviewed to assess and minimize risk.
• These maps help determine whether a site is within a volcanic risk zone and support appropriate
decision-making during the site selection process.

## Slide 33

2.Site Selection
Site Selection Considerations: Natural Hazards
• Wildfire
Introduction
Wildfires can spread rapidly, covering areas of 60 km² (15,000 acres) or more. Even if a data center is not directly in the
path of a wildfire, events occurring as far as 80 km (50 miles) away can still impact the site—particularly through
disruptions to transmission infrastructure or access services.
Wildfires usually happen in remote or rural areas, but sites located near undeveloped land or limited development may
still be at risk for interruption or physical damage.
Recommendations
• Avoid placing data centers at the urban-wildland interface or near protected natural areas.
• Review historical wildfire records and service disruption logs from network or utility providers before selecting a site.
• For sites located in areas of moderate or high wildfire risk, ensure the presence of redundant access routes for both
operational staff and emergency services.
• Implement disaster recovery and evacuation plans to maintain operations and personnel safety if a wildfire evacuation
becomes necessary.

## Slide 34

2.Site Selection
Site Selection Considerations: Natural Hazards
• Flood Plains
Introduction
Flooding can happen in a wide range of locations, including those not typically associated with
high rainfall or snowfall. Risk may come from river overflow, coastal surge, or infrastructure failure.
Recommendations
• The selected site should not be located within a known flood plain or near flood-prone areas
such as tidal basins, dams, tsunami zones, or levees.
• Avoid placing the site within designated flood hazard or tsunami inundation areas per the
International Building Code (IBC).
• The site should be:
⚬At least 91 meters (300 feet) away from a 500-year floodplain, or
⚬At least 3 meters (10 feet) above the highest recorded flood level.
• All access roads leading to the data center should also be elevated to remain usable during a
flood event.
• In some cases, a site-specific flood risk study may be required to confirm safety.

Site Selection Considerations: Natural Environment
• Ground Stability: Landslides
Introduction
Landslides occur when a slope transitions from a stable to an unstable condition. This instability can arise
from various factors such as soil saturation, seismic activity, or erosion. Notably, landslides do not require
steep elevation changes—they can occur even in areas that appear relatively flat due to hidden weaknesses in
the underlying ground structure.
Recommendations
For new building sites, especially data centers, the suitability of the location must be verified through:
• Up-to-date site documentation
• Geological records
• Formal geotechnical analysis or other analytical assessments

## Slide 39

2.Site Selection
Site Selection Considerations: Natural Environment
• Ground Stability: Soil Stability
Introduction
Poor soil stability can cause uneven settling of a data center building, potentially leading to structural
damage. In severe cases, this includes sinkhole formation. Even minor instability can compromise the
structural integrity of the facility.
Recommendations
The following factors should be assessed to ensure proper soil conditions:
• Avoid areas with quicksand-like, unstable, or highly expansive soils.
• Confirm there is no subsurface contamination, either from prior hazardous waste storage or from nearby
industrial activity.
• Ensure there are no underground voids, such as solution-formed cavities in limestone, which could lead to
sinkholes.

## Slide 42

2.Site Selection
Site Selection Considerations: Natural Environment
• Ground Water
Introduction
Groundwater exists in the spaces between soil particles or within cracks in underground rock formations. The
depth at which these spaces become fully saturated is known as the water table. Groundwater naturally rises to
the surface through springs or wetlands and can pose a risk to building stability and infrastructure integrity.
Recommendations
• The selected site should have a low water table, ideally below the lowest part of the building and any
underground utilities.
• For slab-on-grade facilities, build on elevated or flat ground to avoid water intrusion.
• For facilities with below-grade levels (e.g., basements) or that are situated at the bottom of a hill, special
construction measures may be needed to prevent seepage or flooding from seasonal groundwater changes.
• Sites located at low elevations or with poor drainage should be evaluated carefully for water table risks.

## Slide 47

2.Site Selection
Site Selection Considerations: Utility Services
• Power and Electrical Service
⚬Capacity Availability: The site must be served with enough electrical utility capacity to meet current and future needs based on
the data center’s Availability Class.
⚬Recommendations: Multiple independent utility circuits are preferred. Collaborate with a qualified electrical engineer and
utility provider for optimal circuit design.
⚬Unit Substations: Medium voltage substations may be required, typically located outdoors but can be internal in urban sites.
⚬Utility Transformers: Transformers reduce utility voltage to operational levels. They may be pole-mounted, pad-mounted, or
internal.
⚬Proven Utility Reliability: A second utility feed is only recommended when the operational and availability classifications are
both Level 4, and utility MTBF is low (e.g., <50% reliability).
⚬Service Entrance Separation: At least 1.2 meters (4 ft) of separation from other utilities; 20 meters (66 ft) for redundant feeds.
⚬Underground vs Overhead: Underground utility lines are preferred due to greater protection from weather and accidents.
⚬On-Site Generation: Backup and emergency generators should be secured, sized, and fueled to support operations for a
minimum of 48 hours.
⚬Microgrids: On-site energy systems (e.g., solar, wind) may be used to supplement or replace utility feeds, offering resilience
and energy independence.

• Communications
⚬Capacity: Adequate copper and fiber capacity must be provisioned for full site requirements. Multiple paths recommended.
⚬Reliability: Preferably use diversely routed feeds from different access provider central offices.

⚬Backup Water Supply: F3 and F4 data centers must have at least 72–96 hours of water backup capacity for critical cooling
functions.

## Slide 54

3.Operations Management
Service Operations
The main goal of service operations is to ensure that the data center performs according to agreed service
levels. When deviations occur, corrective actions must be taken to restore service compliance.
Key Points:
• Service levels are defined by metrics agreed upon with the business (e.g., uptime).
• Large data centers may track hundreds to thousands of metrics.
• Different teams are needed to monitor and manage these metrics.
• In large organizations, service level management might function as a separate unit.
• The complexity of operations scales with data center size, increasing potential for overload unless properly
structured.

## Slide 57

Incident and Trouble Management
What is an “Incident”?
An incident refers to an event that disrupts normal operations and needs intervention to restore service. However, not all
alerts or events are incidents—only those that can affect service levels and require operator attention qualify.
• Incidents are resolved once normal operations resume (even if the root cause is unknown).
• Any personnel can report incidents, though typically it's done by operational staff.
Role of Trouble Management:
• Focuses on resolving service-impacting hardware/software issues quickly to avoid service level breaches.
• Problem management goes deeper: it analyzes recurring incidents to prevent future disruptions.
Key Takeaway:
Incident management restores service quickly, while problem management prevents future issues. Experienced
operators, often promoted to problem managers, handle this vital function.

General Considerations
The primary goal of airflow management in data centers is to deliver air efficiently to the intake of Information Technology Equipment (ITE) and
return it to the cooling equipment without losses. It is critical to:
⚬Minimize bypass air, which is air that moves from the cooling equipment to the return path without passing through the ITE.
⚬Eliminate recirculation air, where hot exhaust air from the ITE re-enters the intake without being cooled.
Cooling system design must take a holistic approach, considering:
⚬The presence or absence of a raised floor,
⚬The path and control of return air,
⚬Placement of CRAC/CRAH units or other air handlers,
⚬Hot and cold aisle orientation,
⚬Ceiling height and humidity control,
⚬Future expansion needs.
All of these factors influence one another and directly impact the performance of the cooling system.
Industry resources like Datacom Equipment Power Trends and Cooling Applications and ASHRAE Design Considerations provide general guidance
on airflow strategies and cooling technologies. However, they do not offer rigid selection rules due to the wide variety of d ata center designs.
Therefore, successful airflow management requires input from a knowledgeable and experienced data center cooling engineer or consultant.

## Slide 127

4.Cooling Systems
Airflow Management
Airflow Loops and ΔT Behavior
Airflow consists of two independent ("decoupled") loops:
• CRAC loop (few large fans): Circulates room air.
• Server loop (many small fans): Moves air through cold and hot aisles.
Thermal and airflow dynamics:
• Hot air is buoyant and flows upward, returning to CRAC via the path of least resistance.
• Greater distance to CRAC → less momentum → more turbulence.
ΔT (temperature rise) considerations:
• Server ΔT: 11–17 °C (20–30 °F)
• CRAC ΔT: 8–11 °C (15–20 °F)
• CRAC units often need to move ~50% more air than all server fans combined.
Ideal system design:
• Supply air at correct temperature and volume that matches server fans.
• All hot aisle air should return without mixing or bypass.
• When isolation isn't possible, monitor and calibrate CRAC supply air carefully.

## Slide 196

5.Energy Systems
Redundancy
Redundancy refers to additional systems or capacity beyond the minimum (N) needed to support critical loads.
It ensures operations can continue even during component failures or maintenance.
Redundancy Levels Defined
• N (Need or Baseline Requirement)
⚬Minimum capacity needed to support the load.
⚬No redundancy is included.
• N+1 Redundancy
⚬Adds one extra unit/module/path beyond the required minimum.
⚬System can tolerate a single failure or maintenance without interrupting operation.
⚬For small systems, N+1 ≈ 2N (if a single module supports the full load).
• N+2 Redundancy
⚬Adds two extra units/modules/paths beyond the base requirement.
⚬Protects against two simultaneous failures.

---

## Slide 197

5.Energy Systems
Redundancy
• 2N Redundancy
⚬Provides two full sets of equipment (modules, paths, etc.) for each required one.
⚬Also known as dual-path topology.
⚬Fully fault-tolerant: failure of an entire path or system won't disrupt operations.
⚬In smaller systems, 2N ≈ N+1 if the modules support full load independently.
• 2(N+1) Redundancy
⚬Two complete N+1 systems.
⚬Even if one module in each system fails, full redundancy is retained.
• Multi-N Redundancy (xN)
⚬Used in very large or fault-tolerant systems.
⚬More than two large systems operate in parallel.
⚬Redundancy is achieved at the PDU or branch circuit level.
Important Notes
• “N”, N+1, N+2, etc. should not be used alone to define a redundancy Class.
• The context (e.g., component vs. system level) affects redundancy classification.
• Instead, use BICSI Classes or defined performance characteristics for precise redundancy levels.

## Slide 215

Automatic, Single-Step Response
A UPS must stabilize quickly after a failure. If transition involves too many steps, critical power loss may

issues in designs (especially ring-bus) must be avoided.

failure/maintenance modes.
Stored Energy System
UPS must carry the full critical ITE load during any power failure, including while switching to generator or
other backup sources. This includes support for cooling and environmental controls essential to IT
operations.

• Inrush currents
• Continuous-duty compliance (e.g., NEC standards)

Design assumes full utility/generator support.
Scalability
System architecture must support phased capacity growth with properly sized modules—not too small or
oversized for the Class goal.

## Slide 230

6.Power Systems
Automatic Transfer Switches (ATS)
Introduction
• Automatic Transfer Switches (ATS) are electromechanical devices that automatically switch a data center’s electrical load
from a primary (preferred) power source (such as utility) to a secondary (backup) source (such as a generator) when the
primary fails.
• Once the preferred source is restored and stable, the ATS can automatically retransfer the load back.
• Unlike ASTSs, which are solid-state and operate within milliseconds, traditional ATSs operate with mechanical contacts and
take a few seconds to complete the transfer.
• Commonly found in:
⚬Generator-backed systems
⚬Facilities where IT loads tolerate brief interruptions
⚬Scenarios where cost or complexity doesn't justify solid-state switching
• Typical use cases include:
⚬Tier I and Tier II data centers
⚬Facility power infrastructure (cooling systems, lighting, etc.)
⚬Supporting non-critical IT infrastructure

---

## Slide 231

6.Power Systems
Automatic Transfer Switches (ATS)
Working Principle
• The ATS continuously monitors voltage, frequency, and phase of the primary power source.
• If the primary source fails or drifts outside acceptable parameters, the ATS initiates a break-before-make
operation:
a. Disconnects the primary source.
b. Connects to the backup source.
c. Retransfers back once the preferred source is stable again.
• Transfer time usually ranges from 2 to 10 seconds, which is acceptable for non-sensitive loads, but not
ideal for critical computing loads that demand zero downtime.

## Slide 234

6.Power Systems
Automatic Static Transfer Switches (ASTS)
Introduction
• ASTSs are used in data center power systems where rapid, subcycle switching between power sources is needed and mechanical alternatives
are too slow.
• ASTS functions similarly to Automatic Transfer Switches (ATS), but it uses solid-state components to allow for very fast (subcycle) transfers:
⚬Transfers the load automatically from the preferred source to the alternative source when a failure occurs.
⚬Returns to the preferred source once it is restored.
• ASTSs are crucial when:
⚬Loads are single- or dual-corded (most IT equipment).
⚬Newer systems with multiple power cords (3, 4, 5+) benefit from the path redundancy ASTS offers without requiring a complete
duplication of UPS infrastructure.
• Common non-ITE critical loads requiring ASTS include:
⚬Temperature and building monitoring systems
⚬Video/control systems
⚬Security systems
⚬Single-corded IT devices
• Not typically used in Class F0, F1, or F2 setups where UPS power distribution paths are already redundant and single-path static switches
could present a risk.

## Slide 267

7.Power Distribution Strategies
Input Source Transfer
Open Transition
Introduction
In this method, the load experiences a brief interruption in power as the system disconnects from one source
before connecting to another. It is the most widely used technique due to its simplicity, low cost, and lack of
utility approval requirements. However, it imposes temporary stress on UPS batteries and downstream
mechanical systems like chillers or air conditioners, which must restart after the transition.
Recommendations
• Ensure transitions last several seconds to allow for full energy dissipation in capacitive circuits and to
minimize voltage sags.

## Slide 268

Input Source Transfer
Closed Transition/Quick Transfer
Introduction
Quick transfer systems momentarily parallel utility and generator sources for a period ranging from 100
milliseconds to one minute. This enables a seamless transition without power interruption. It is particularly
useful for sites requiring high reliability. However, it adds design complexity due to:
• Short-circuit duty from combined utility and generator sources
• Source synchronization challenges
• Potential for transient feedback causing logic malfunctions
• Additional utility approvals in customer-owned systems
Recommendations
• Engage utility providers early in the design process.
• Model synchronization and short circuit conditions carefully.

## Slide 299

7.Power Distribution Strategies
Automatic Transfer Switches (ATS)
Introduction
• Automatic Transfer Switches (ATS) are electromechanical devices that automatically switch a data center’s electrical load
from a primary (preferred) power source (such as utility) to a secondary (backup) source (such as a generator) when the
primary fails.
• Once the preferred source is restored and stable, the ATS can automatically retransfer the load back.
• Unlike ASTSs, which are solid-state and operate within milliseconds, traditional ATSs operate with mechanical contacts and
take a few seconds to complete the transfer.
• Commonly found in:
⚬Generator-backed systems
⚬Facilities where IT loads tolerate brief interruptions
⚬Scenarios where cost or complexity doesn't justify solid-state switching
• Typical use cases include:
⚬Tier I and Tier II data centers
⚬Facility power infrastructure (cooling systems, lighting, etc.)
⚬Supporting non-critical IT infrastructure

---

## Slide 300

7.Power Distribution Strategies
Automatic Transfer Switches (ATS)
Working Principle
• The ATS continuously monitors voltage, frequency, and phase of the primary power source.
• If the primary source fails or drifts outside acceptable parameters, the ATS initiates a break-before-make
operation:
a. Disconnects the primary source.
b. Connects to the backup source.
c. Retransfers back once the preferred source is stable again.
• Transfer time usually ranges from 2 to 10 seconds, which is acceptable for non-sensitive loads, but not
ideal for critical computing loads that demand zero downtime.

• Delay timers and retransfer logic to prevent unnecessary switching

• Remote monitoring and alarms in advanced models
